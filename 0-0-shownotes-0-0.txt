What really stands out is how fast he didn’t just learn, but leveled up. He’s already releasing his own assets into the world, making them available to the community, and someone has already purchased one. That’s not a hypothetical win. That’s real proof that his work has value.

And instead of keeping that momentum to himself, he’s become someone people trust. People are actively reaching out to him, asking questions, booking time with him, and wanting him to teach them because he knows his stuff and explains it well. That shift from learner to mentor does not happen by accident. It happens because someone puts in the time and earns that respect.

What we’re seeing here feels like the beginning of something much bigger. Bigjon is building real, transferable skills, building confidence, building a reputation, and opening the door to making money doing creative work in VR. This is the kind of path where you look back in a year and go, “That’s where it started.”

Bigjon, you should be incredibly proud of what you’ve accomplished already, and we’re all excited to see where you take this next. This is absolutely a win worth celebrating.

Darwin Award

Alright, this one is pure gold. And by gold, I mean “somebody actually did this and somehow lived to tell the tale.”

In Nigeria, a young man decided he needed some extra protection in life. Not like wearing a helmet, not like paying attention, not like staying away from obviously dangerous situations. No. He went straight to the ultimate confidence booster: a bulletproof charm. A little magical talisman that the local traditional healer guaranteed would stop any bullet cold. Nothing, and I mean nothing, could touch him. Physics? Overrated. Common sense? Optional.

But here’s where it gets truly spectacular. The man didn’t just buy the charm to feel safe while walking around. Oh no. He needed proof. Hard evidence. A little “let’s see if this works in real life” demonstration. Because nothing screams “I am smart and prepared” like testing magical immunity with a loaded gun.

So he tells his friend, who is apparently either equally optimistic or equally lacking in judgment, to shoot him. Yes, he literally says, “Go ahead, pull the trigger. The charm will save me.”

And his friend, doing his civic duty to science or maybe just being bad at life, does exactly that.
---
Now, spoiler alert: the charm did not work. Shocking, I know. The bullet hit him like a very firm reminder that reality exists, physics is not negotiable, and magical thinking will not protect you from bullets. His mystical confidence? Useless. His investment in mystical protection? Totally worthless. And the universe? Quietly laughing somewhere offstage.

Here’s the thing that makes it truly Darwin‑esque. He survived. Somehow. Emergency services arrived, patched him up, and probably asked questions like, “Why are you lying on the floor with a bullet in you?” But he lived. And I imagine at some point in the hospital, he realized that maybe, just maybe, standing in front of a gun and trusting a charm was not the most reliable life strategy.

And the healer? Still alive. Probably still selling charms to the next person who thinks bullets are negotiable. Honestly, that guy is winning at life.

So the lesson here is simple. If a stranger tells you a charm will make you bulletproof, you have exactly two options. One: don’t buy it. Or two: don’t be the guy who tests it with an actual gun pointed at you. Pick your poison.
