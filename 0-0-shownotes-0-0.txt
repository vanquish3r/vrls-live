This Week’s Bright Spot

Quick note before we get into this. Over time, we’ve highlighted a huge portion of the community through Community Spotlight. As we’ve started circling back toward repeats, it felt like the right moment to evolve the segment rather than just rerun the same format.

So Community Spotlight is becoming This Week’s Bright Spot.

Same heart, same intention, just a wider lens. This gives us room to celebrate wins of all kinds. Big, small, personal, professional, or just moments that deserve a little recognition. Because this community isn’t only what happens in VR, it’s the people behind the headsets too.

This week’s bright spot is Dutch.

Dutch just earned a promotion at work, and honestly, it makes complete sense to anyone who knows him. He’s consistent. He’s reliable. He shows up, does the work, and does it without needing to be the loudest person in the room. He’s the kind of person people trust because he follows through.

He brings that same energy everywhere. Steady, supportive, thoughtful, and quietly competent. The kind of person who makes things run smoother just by being involved. Those are the people who don’t always get flashy praise, but they’re the ones teams depend on.

A promotion like this isn’t luck. It’s a reflection of character, work ethic, and the respect he’s earned over time.

So Dutch, congratulations. We see you, we’re proud of you, and this win couldn’t have gone to a better person.

Let’s give it up for Dutch!

Explain Your Avatar Like a Dating Profile

Core Premise

People describe their avatar as if they are desperately trying to attract someone on a dating app. Everything is framed as a selling point, even things that are clearly red flags.

The Rules (Say These Out Loud)

You are describing your avatar, not yourself
You must oversell everything
You must completely ignore any red flags
Confidence is mandatory

How It Runs Live

Pick a volunteer or call on someone randomly
Ask them to describe their avatar like a dating profile bio
Let them finish without interruption
Hosts react by gently translating what was actually said

Darwin Award

This one happens every winter. Every single winter. Which is wild, because by now carbon monoxide should have its own publicist.

This took place during a deep freeze in the US Midwest. The kind of cold where your car makes sounds it has never made before and your soul briefly leaves your body when you open the door.

A man decided to go out late at night and warm up his car. Totally normal winter behaviour. Except standing outside while it warms up is miserable, and misery makes people creative in the worst way.

So he started the car in his garage.

The garage door was closed.

Now in his mind, this was not a bad idea. This was a temporary idea. Just a few minutes. In and out. No problem. Because if there’s one thing carbon monoxide is known for, it’s respecting time limits.

He went back inside, sat down on the couch, and turned on the TV while the car idled away in the garage like a silent little assassin.

Carbon monoxide has no smell, no colour, no drama. It doesn’t kick the door in. It doesn’t announce itself. It just slowly fills the space and makes you feel kind of sleepy, which is extremely rude.

A few minutes go by. He starts feeling tired. Heavy. Maybe a little foggy. Which he blames on winter, life, and probably the news.
---
Then he passes out.

No panic. No warning. Just lights out.

Meanwhile, outside, a neighbour is walking their dog. Because dogs do not care how cold it is, ever. The neighbour notices something weird. A car running. Garage door closed. Midnight. Vibes are off.

This neighbour, and I cannot stress this enough, had carbon monoxide safety training. Which is the only reason this story doesn’t end very differently.

They call emergency services immediately.

First responders force entry, ventilate the house, and drag this man out of his own living room. Doctors later confirm that his carbon monoxide levels were high enough that if he had stayed there a little longer, he would not have woken up.

He survived. No lasting physical damage. Just a hospital stay, a headache, and the kind of humiliation that installs multiple carbon monoxide detectors out of pure shame.

Fire departments talk about this exact scenario every winter. Not as a rare freak accident. As a greatest hit.

So somewhere out there is a man who almost died because he wanted his car to be warm and his feet to be indoors.

Winter does not care about convenience. And carbon monoxide does not care about your plans.

Things We Pretend Are Normal

Alright, let’s talk about the weird things we all just accept in VR. Things that absolutely would be insane in real life… but here? Totally fine.

* Standing inside someone else and calling it “personal space”
* Jumping five metres in the air with no explanation
* Teleporting through walls like it’s a casual commute
* Spinning in circles endlessly because “it feels fun”
* Sitting on air, pretending it’s a chair
* Randomly changing avatars mid-conversation

Ask co-host to name some, then audience.
