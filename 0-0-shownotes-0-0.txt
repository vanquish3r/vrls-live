Colbert

I want to take a minute to acknowledge The Late Show with Stephen Colbert, because whether people realize it or not, this is a huge loss.

Colbert was genuinely one of the reasons I ever had the thought of doing a late show at all. Not because I thought I was going to be Stephen Colbert in VR, obviously, but because there was something about the format that stuck with me. The humour, the intelligence, the timing, the way he could take the absolute circus of the world and somehow turn it into something people could laugh at, think about, and maybe feel a little less alone inside.

That matters.

And I don’t think people always understand how much work goes into making a show happen. Every episode of something like *The Late Show* looks smooth when you’re watching it, but behind that is an entire world of people making it happen. Writers, crew, producers, editors, researchers, musicians, tech people, guests, and everyone else who keeps that machine moving night after night.

And no, we are not a nightly network show. We are a VR late show held together by two people on stage, one man behind the curtain, a potentially drunk cameraman, and a voice in the darkness of the audience. A lot of ideas, a lot of effort, and the occasional prayer to the gods of “please let the world function tonight.” We don’t have some massive production team behind us. A lot of the planning, timing, structure, writing, and making-it-all-work chaos falls on me and my co-host.
---
So in our own much smaller, much weirder way, we do understand that shows don’t just appear. They are built. They are carried by people who care. They take energy. They take thought. They take showing up even when you’re tired, even when the world is messy, even when there’s too much happening and you still have to find a way to make people laugh.

So when I think about The Late Show ending, I don’t just think about Colbert behind the desk. I think about the entire ecosystem of people who helped create something sharp, funny, thoughtful, and important for years. That is a massive amount of creative work. That is a lot of people losing a platform, a job, a space, and a piece of what they helped build.

And yeah, I also think we need to talk about what this feels like politically, because pretending otherwise feels ridiculous.

There will be statements. There will be careful corporate language. There will be some polished explanation about finances or ratings or whatever makes it all sound clean and normal. And maybe parts of that are true. But let’s not act like this is happening in a vacuum.

Because when one of the loudest political satirists on television disappears during a time when powerful people are getting more hostile toward criticism, that is not nothing.
---
When comedy starts making the wrong people uncomfortable, suddenly everyone finds a “business reason” to get nervous. And I’m sorry, but that’s some fucking bullshit.

You don’t have to agree with every joke. You don’t have to watch every monologue. You don’t even have to like Stephen Colbert. But the second we start accepting that people in power should be shielded from criticism, parody, and mockery, we are walking into dangerous territory with a blindfold on and a corporate-approved smile.

Satire is supposed to punch up. Late-night comedy is supposed to look at the chaos and say, “No, we are not pretending this is normal.” It’s supposed to hold up a mirror to corruption, hypocrisy, cruelty, and absurdity, and sometimes that mirror is funny because if we didn’t laugh, we would probably just scream into a pillow until 2028.

That’s why this matters. It’s not just a show ending. Shows end. Hosts leave. Television changes. That part happens.
---
But this feels bigger than that. It feels like another space where criticism is being squeezed. Another place where sharp edges are being sanded down. Another reminder that the people with the most power are often the most fragile when someone dares to make a joke at their expense. And that should bother us.

Because comedy has always been one of the ways people push back. It’s one of the ways people survive the insanity of the world. It lets people say the thing everyone is thinking but is too exhausted, scared, or careful to say out loud.

Stephen Colbert did that for a long time. He made people laugh. He made people think. He pissed off the right people. And honestly, that last part is usually how you know someone is doing something important.

So yeah, I’m sad about it. I’m angry about it. And I think it’s bullshit. But mostly, I think it’s worth acknowledging what we’re losing. Not just a host. Not just a show. But a whole creative force that kept showing up, kept speaking, kept joking, and kept pushing back in a world that keeps trying very hard to make everyone quieter.

Stephen Colbert inspired a lot of people. He inspired me. And late-night comedy should never become something power gets to silence just because it doesn’t like the punchline. We hope you enjoy tonights show, as we've definitely put a Colbert spin on things.

Darwin Award

This week’s Darwin Award is a little different, because in honour of tonight’s Colbert tribute, we are giving out what I’m going to call a Darwin Honourable Mention. Because technically, nobody died. Technically, nobody removed themselves from the gene pool. Technically, this was a planned comedy bit with professionals involved, which means there were probably permits, safety people, and at least one poor production assistant standing there with a clipboard silently questioning every life choice that led them to rooftop watermelon duty. But spiritually? Spiritually, this is Darwin-adjacent.

Because this week, Stephen Colbert and David Letterman apparently decided that the healthy, emotionally balanced way to process the end of The Late Show was to go up onto the roof of the Ed Sullivan Theater and start throwing CBS office furniture, watermelons, and a cake off the building. And honestly? Respect. Because there are a lot of ways to say goodbye to a network. You could give a heartfelt speech. You could do a touching montage. You could thank the crew, wave to the audience, maybe cry a little, hug somebody famous, and walk off into late-night history with dignity. Or, and hear me out, you could take a watermelon to the roof and say, “This one’s for corporate.”

That is not closure. That is produce-based therapy. That is the five stages of grief if one of the stages was “launching office supplies at a giant CBS logo.” And I love that it was Colbert and Letterman together, because that makes it feel less like a reckless stunt and more like late-night royalty conducting a very specific religious ceremony. Like, you know somewhere, an intern was told, “Mr. Letterman and Mr. Colbert need three chairs, two watermelons, and a cake on the roof.” And that intern had to just nod like, “Of course. Television.” No follow-up questions. Because once you work in entertainment long enough, you stop asking why two grown men in suits need baked goods at rooftop altitude. You just go find the cake and hope it’s not union.
---
And can we talk about the cake? The furniture makes sense. The watermelons make sense in a very stupid, satisfying, Gallagher-had-a-law-degree kind of way. But the cake? That feels personal. Nobody throws a cake unless something has gone deeply wrong in their spirit. Cake is supposed to be for birthdays, weddings, celebrations, and passive-aggressive office goodbyes where someone named Karen cuts the pieces too small. So if the cake is going off the roof, that is no longer dessert. That is a statement. That cake was not thrown. That cake was released from its contract.

And the furniture? That’s where this starts getting dangerously close to a workplace safety video. Because any time the phrase “two men threw office furniture off a roof” appears in a sentence, there is a very thin line between “comedy bit” and “incident report.” Like, this is exactly the kind of thing HR sees and immediately starts drafting an email that begins with, “Just a reminder that rooftops are not approved disposal areas.”

And honestly, imagine being the CBS logo at the bottom. Just sitting there, minding your business, representing a major television network, and suddenly here comes a watermelon from the sky at terminal spite. That logo didn’t even have time to process. One second it’s corporate branding. The next second it’s in a Gallagher sketch directed by the ghost of free speech.

And that’s why this gets the Darwin Honourable Mention. Not because it was stupid in a bad way. Not because anyone actually got hurt. But because if you hear “Colbert and Letterman threw furniture, watermelons, and cake off the roof of the Ed Sullivan Theater,” and your immediate response is not, “Okay, but how close did we come to a very classy New York felony?” then you are not appreciating the full cinematic danger of the moment.
---
This could have gone wrong in so many ways. A chair could have caught the wind. A watermelon could have bounced with malicious intent. The cake could have achieved sentience. David Letterman could have looked into the camera and said, “You know, Stephen, I think we can get the desk up here.” And then suddenly we’re not watching a comedy bit anymore. We’re watching two legends invent late-night parkour.

But instead, it became exactly what it needed to be. Absurd. Petty. Symbolic. Beautiful. A perfect little act of controlled chaos from two people who understand that sometimes the most honest way to say goodbye is not with a speech. Sometimes it’s with a watermelon.

So congratulations to Stephen Colbert and David Letterman. You do not receive a full Darwin Award, because everyone survived, and apparently the rooftop gods were merciful. But you do receive this week’s Darwin Honourable Mention for looking at the end of an era and saying, “We could process this like adults… or we could throw CBS furniture into the void.” And frankly, gentlemen? No notes.
